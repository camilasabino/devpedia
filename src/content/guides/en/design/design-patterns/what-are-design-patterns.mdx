A design pattern is a reusable solution to a problem that comes up again and again when designing software. It isn't an algorithm (an exact recipe of steps) or a library (code you import and use as-is), but something in between: a way of structuring classes and objects that solves a recurring problem, and that you have to adapt to each concrete case. Nobody "installs" the Observer pattern; you implement it, with names and details specific to each project, following a general shape that's already known to work.

That general shape didn't come out of nowhere: in 1994, four authors — known ever since as the "Gang of Four" (GoF) — published a catalog of 23 patterns they'd seen recur in well-designed object-oriented systems. That catalog, with slight variations, is still the standard reference.

## Why they're worth knowing

- **They're proven solutions.** When you use a known pattern, you aren't inventing a new solution to a design problem — you're applying something that has been validated across thousands of different projects. That reduces the margin for error in decisions that, made badly, are expensive to reverse later.
- **They give you shared vocabulary.** Saying "this needs a Strategy" communicates a complete design intent in three words to another developer who knows the catalog. Without that vocabulary, the same idea means explaining the whole structure every time.
- **They make a future change possible without touching code that already works.** Most patterns exist so that one kind of change (adding a new type of product, a new export format, a new business rule) gets resolved by adding new code, not by modifying code that already works and has already been tested.

## Not every problem needs a pattern

Patterns aren't a goal in themselves. Dropping a pattern in where it isn't needed adds indirection, files, and layers that someone later has to understand — sometimes the simplest solution is to use no pattern at all. The useful question isn't "can I use a pattern here?" but "has someone already solved this specific problem in a recognizable way?". Each pattern's article includes a section on when it's worth it and when it isn't, exactly for that.

- **[Creational patterns](/en/design/patterns/creational-patterns/)** — about _how_ objects get created, so the code isn't tied to concrete classes.
- **[Structural patterns](/en/design/patterns/structural-patterns/)** — about how classes and objects combine into larger structures while staying flexible and efficient.
- **[Behavioral patterns](/en/design/patterns/behavioral-patterns/)** — about how responsibilities are divided among objects and how they communicate with each other.
- **[Design patterns that are often confused](/en/design/patterns/commonly-confused-patterns/)** — some patterns have similar structures but solve different problems: what sets them apart and how to recognize when to use each.

## The domain of the examples

The code examples in this topic are in Java, and almost all of them share one fictional case: **AndesShop**, an e-commerce store selling clothing and trekking gear that keeps growing and runs into different design problems as it adds features.

AndesShop is the same business whose payments module the [software architecture topic](/en/architecture/) uses as its example: there it's viewed from the outside, deciding the boundaries between services, what data each one shares, and how they communicate; here it's viewed from inside a class. That's deliberate: design patterns and architecture decisions operate on the same system, at different scales.

## Relationship with design principles

A pattern isn't an alternative to the [design principles](/en/design/principles/), it's a concrete application of several of them at once. Strategy is [Open/Closed](/en/design/principles/solid/#o--openclosed-principle) made structural; Decorator is [composition over inheritance](/en/design/principles/general-design-principles/#composition-over-inheritance) taken to its limit; almost all of them push the dependency toward an abstraction, which is [dependency inversion](/en/design/principles/solid/#d--dependency-inversion-principle).

That's why it's worth reading the principles before the catalog: patterns make a lot more sense once you know which design tension they're resolving.
